# Bug Terrarium — Game Concept

## The pitch

A living terrarium of procedurally-generated bugs. Every bug is genetically
unique — no two look or perform exactly alike. You breed them, raise them, and
learn who they are the same way you'd get to know a real pet: by watching,
not by reading a stat sheet.

## The core idea

Each bug is built from a set of "genes" — body shape, legs, wings, jaws,
armor, color, and more. Those genes quietly determine everything about how
the bug looks *and* how it plays: how fast it moves, how hard it hits, how
well it hides, whether it can fly.

But you never get to see those numbers. There's no menu that says "Attack: 74."
Genes and stats stay hidden — you find out what a bug is capable of by putting
it in situations and watching what happens. A bug with huge mandibles bites
hard but slow. A bug covered in spines shrugs off hits. You won't be told
that — you'll notice it.

## Why hide the numbers?

Most breeding/collecting games eventually turn into a spreadsheet exercise:
check the stats, pick the best rolls, discard the rest. That's efficient, but
it kills the sense of discovery.

Here, without exact numbers to optimize against, players end up relating to
their bugs more like actual animals — this one's a scrapper, that one's a
coward, this one's freakishly fast for no reason you can point to on a chart.
Two players can walk away with a totally different read on the "best" bug in
their terrarium, because they each learned it through their own experience
with it, not from a number.

## How you actually learn about a bug

- **Watch it live.** Bugs behave in the terrarium on their own — wandering,
  fighting, reacting to day and night. A lot becomes obvious just from
  paying attention.
- **Fight and train.** Combat and training reveal a bug's strengths and
  weaknesses through outcomes, not readouts. You'll feel that a bug is fast
  or tough because of what happens in a fight, not because a bar told you so.
- **Visit the vet station.** The one place you can actually *see* a bug's
  genetics — but only visually, never as numbers. Bring a bug in, and you get
  a clean, close-up look at it: every physical trait rendered clearly, no
  distractions. It tells you what the bug *is*, not what it's *worth*. And
  taking a bug there costs time — it's unavailable during the visit, and
  can't be brought back immediately after, so it's a deliberate choice, not
  something you'd do constantly.

## Breeding

Bugs breed to produce offspring that blend their parents' genes, with a bit
of random mutation thrown in — so lineages drift and diversify over
generations rather than converging on one "perfect" bug. Because you're
judging bugs by feel rather than by numbers, breeding decisions become about
which bugs *seem* right to keep pairing, not about chasing a maxed-out stat
line.

## The vibe

Think somewhere between an ant farm, a terrarium you'd actually keep on a
shelf, and a creature-collecting game — but slower, more observational, and
built around curiosity instead of optimization. The day/night cycle runs on
your real clock, so it feels less like a session you play and more like a
small world that's always going, whether you're watching or not.
